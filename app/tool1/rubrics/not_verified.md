# Unverified items, limits, and deliberate omissions

Version 0.3, 2026-10-16. Updated for the five-level scale in both rubrics. In v0.2, S4 (Hattie & Timperley) and S5 (Shute) were removed from the feedback rubric and S6 to S8 added (DECISIONS.md D1). This file lists everything in the two rubric drafts that I could not confirm, and everything I chose to leave out. It is meant to guide the team's review.

## 1. Quotes withheld

The task asked for a verbatim quote of up to 25 words for every use of a source. I am bound by a rule against reproducing copyrighted text from documents I read, beyond a single short quotation, so `sources_verification.md` gives a precise locator and a close paraphrase in place of each quote. Each passage should be checked by opening the paper at the stated page, column, heading, and paragraph. Until a team member does that, treat each paraphrase as my reading of the passage.

## 2. Sources and copies

- **S2 peer review not confirmed.** The publisher page for *Studies in Business and Economics* did not say whether the journal is peer reviewed, and I did not verify its review policy. S2 is also a single cross-sectional survey of 142 workers recruited on Mechanical Turk [S2 pp.131 to 132], which the authors list as a limitation [S2 p.136]. It is the weakest source in the set.
- **Copies read were not publisher copies.** S1 came from a university course site, S6 to S8 from team-supplied ProQuest copies, and S3 from a third-party teaching site whose authorization I could not confirm. S3 is a scanned PDF with a machine-read text layer. The table numbers I used from S3 (Table 2, p.273) read cleanly, but the team should check them against the publisher version.
- **DOI checks.** Two publisher sites (Sage, APA PsycNet) blocked automated access. I confirmed those DOIs through Crossref metadata (title, authors, journal, volume, issue, pages). I did not view the publisher landing pages for S1 or S3. For S6 to S8, S10, and S11, the DOI and journal details were read from each article's first page and were not checked against Crossref.

## 3. Claims resting on secondary reports

These claims appear in the rubrics only as reported by the source I read. I did not open the original works.

- Removed in v0.2. D3 no longer relies on the Latham, Erez, and Locke (1988) finding as reported in S1 p.708. The team ruled the original out as a lab study, and in the comparison I checked, tell versus tell-and-sell, performance did not differ significantly (15.59 vs 15.49, F = 0.18, Tables 7 to 9, pp.762–763). S1 p.708 states the finding more strongly than that comparison supports.
- The statement that time guidelines reduce uncertainty comes from Mayfield and Mayfield (2016) as reported in S2 p.127. It supports D2.
- The core description of motivating language theory (Sullivan, 1988) and the motivating language scale (Mayfield, Mayfield, & Kopf, 1995) comes from S2's summary [S2 p.126]. I tried to open the original scale items and could not reach them. No rubric element relies on the scale items.

## 4. Field of the sources

The task asked for grounding in published communication research. S2 comes from motivating language theory, which S2 places in leader communication [S2 p.126]. S1 and S3 are from psychology journals. S6 (*Social Behavior and Personality*), S7 (*South African Journal of Business Management*), and S8 (*Frontiers in Psychology*) are workplace studies in psychology and management journals. All study communication from managers (goals, instructions, feedback), but none is a communication journal in the narrow sense. Whether they meet the team's definition of communication research is for the team to decide.

## 5. Translation from research to message text

None of the sources scores the text of a written workplace message. S1 studies properties of goals, S2 measures employee perceptions of leader language, S3 pools experiments on feedback interventions across many settings, and S6 to S8 survey employees' general perceptions of their supervisor's feedback. Every scale descriptor turns a construct into features observable in a message, and that step is team judgment. It has to be tested in the pilot with human raters before anyone claims the rubric measures the constructs.

S2 describes motivating language as oral communication [S2 p.126]. Its use for written messages is an assumption.

## 6. Q12 links

No source mentions Q12. Every Q12 link in the drafts rests on overlap between a source's description of a construct and the wording of a Q12 item in G1. Each link is labeled team judgment in the rubrics.

- **Unconfirmed candidate left out.** D3 (rationale) may bear on Q08 (purpose and importance of the job), through S2's description of meaning-making language [S2 p.126]. D3 now rests on S9's trust and evaluation findings, which do not concern job purpose, so I did not assert the link.
- **Q04 tension.** S3 found smaller performance effects when feedback included praise [S3 p.273]. S7 found that perceived positive supervisor feedback predicted higher supervisor-rated performance [S7 p.5]. The two measure praise differently. The split between person-level praise and task-specific praise, which v0.1 based on S4, now has no source and is team judgment. None of these tests recognition's effect on engagement. The drafts do not resolve this. A recognition message rubric would need its own research.

## 7. Elements deliberately left out

| Element | Reason |
|---|---|
| Goal difficulty (task instruction) | Difficulty depends on the reader's ability, which the message does not show. S1 treats it separately from specificity [S1 p.706]. |
| Goal commitment and participation (task instruction) | S1 reports conflicting findings on participation [S1 p.708], and S10 found no reliable difference by participation style [S10 p.1294]. A single message rarely shows whether a goal was set jointly. |
| Expressed confidence in the reader (task instruction) | Has a basis in S1 p.708, but I left it out to keep the draft small. It is a candidate for the team. |
| Empathetic language (both) | Described in S2 p.126, but S2's evidence is perception-level only. It is a candidate for the team. |
| Feedback timing (feedback) | A single message does not show its timing. With S5 removed, no remaining source is cited for the timing evidence, so the exclusion is team judgment. |
| Feedback frequency (feedback) | S3 notes the effect may be an artifact [S3 p.274], and a single message does not show frequency. |
| Oral versus written delivery (feedback) | A property of the channel [S3 p.273]. Every message Tool 1 scores is written. |
| Other message types | I did not open research adequate for recognition messages, project and shift handoffs, change announcements, meeting follow-ups, or requests for input within this task. |

## 8. Elements that are team judgment throughout

These have no source and are labeled as such in the drafts. They are untested.

- The 1 to 5 scale and the choice to write a descriptor and an anchor at all five levels. The level 2 and level 4 descriptors were added in v0.3. Whether raters and the judge can tell adjacent levels apart is tested in the pilot.
- The items moved down a level in v0.3. These are "asap" with no window (D2 level 2), importance asserted with no use (D3 level 2), "keep me posted" with no time (D5 level 2), and an implied standard (F3 level 2).
- The instruction that the judge quotes evidence and writes its note before giving each score, and the matching field order in the output format.
- The wording of every scale descriptor (paraphrases of source constructs, but the levels are mine).
- The label rules, the gating dimensions, and every cutoff.
- The abstention rules, including the threshold of about 8 words.
- The applicability rule for D4.
- The D3 scoring rules (purpose clause only, splitting sentences shared with D1, reasons naming no specific use capped at 3, importance-only reasons scored 2).
- The extra weight on discouraging wording relative to coworker comparisons in F5. Since v0.2 no source supports penalizing coworker comparisons at all.
- The F2 rule that length never raises a score (v0.1 rested it on S5).
- The F1 split between person-level praise and task-specific praise (v0.1 rested it on S4).
- The Q12 links for F3 (Q11) and F4 (Q06, Q12), now based on wording overlap with S1 and S8.
- The judge instructions on ignoring typos, tone, and length.
- The output format.
- The pending status and pending label for writer placeholders such as "[due date?]", added in v1.1 (DECISIONS.md F1 row 3a).
- Every anchor message. All are illustrative and written for this draft.

## 9a. Limits of the sources added in v0.2

- S9 pools lab and field studies [S9 p.431]. The page where S9 defines informational justice was not recorded, so the D3 definition cites the results table only.
- S10 compares group goals. Its rationale comparison rests almost entirely on lab studies [S10 p.1295].
- S11 studies farm business owners filing a government form, and its outcome is timing, not task performance.

- S6 is cross-sectional and self-reported, from 240 employees in South Korea [S6 pp.4, 8]. It links specificity to acceptance and satisfaction, not to performance.
- S7 is time-lagged with supervisor-rated performance, from two manufacturers in China [S7 p.4]. Its positive feedback measure rates the supervisor in general.
- S8 is cross-sectional, from one telecom firm in China [S8 pp.6, 11]. Its outcome is creativity, and its definition of developmental feedback is taken from Zhou (2003), which I did not open.
